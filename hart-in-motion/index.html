The problem

Every town has a story worth carrying further.

Most small towns have the same quiet problem. The history is real, the buildings are standing, the people who love the place are generous with their time, and almost none of it reaches anyone who isn’t already standing in front of it. A brochure, a Facebook page, a museum with limited hours. Good things, all of them, but rarely enough to make a stranger care.

Hart, in Motion is our answer. It’s a personal field trip through Hart, Michigan: two log cabins, a church, a mill, a railway, and a downtown glowing under string lights, all explorable in a browser.

Some places are part of who we are. The work is making sure other people can feel that too.

The trust

Honesty is the feature.

Here is what we think makes this work for towns, not just for fans of 3D. The site says plainly what it knows and what it doesn’t. Renders are labeled as interpretations, not measured replicas. Photographs are kept separate from archival claims. Unverified dates stay open. Each historical note links back to a local source, and the page states when those sources were last checked.

It doesn’t pretend a rendering is a record.

That honesty matters to the people who guard a town’s history. Historical societies and preservation groups can say yes to this because it respects their work instead of overwriting it.

The method

Five steps, always in this order.

  1. Begin with the people. Whose story anchors the collection, and what do they want carried forward?
  2. Photograph the place. Real images are the visual record everything else is measured against.
  3. Interpret carefully. AI-assisted stills and approximate 3D models, always labeled as such.
  4. Link every claim. Local sources, check dates, and open questions kept visible.
  5. Hand it back. A shareable site your town can point to, present, and grow.
